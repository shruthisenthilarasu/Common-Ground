Open challenge · Pilot example
Volunteer Scheduling & Coordination
Nonprofit Open for builders
Riverside Community Kitchen
Nonprofit · San Antonio, TX
Free meals & grocery assistance
~200 volunteers per week
Background

Riverside Community Kitchen coordinates approximately 200 volunteers each week across three programs: meal preparation, food distribution, and community outreach.

The organization has grown significantly over the past two years. Its volunteer management process has not.

Current workflow
  • Volunteers fill out a Google Form expressing interest
  • Responses are manually copied into a spreadsheet
  • Staff assign shifts by hand
  • Volunteers receive individual confirmation emails
  • Reminder emails are sent one by one
  • Hours are tracked in a separate spreadsheet
The problem

This process takes 6–8 hours of staff time every week. When someone cancels last minute, finding a replacement means phone calls and individual emails.

Volunteers report frustration: shifts are hard to view, communication is inconsistent, and opportunities aren't always visible until they're filled.

What success looks like
  • Volunteer opportunities are easy to browse and sign up for
  • Reminders go out automatically
  • Hours are tracked without manual entry
  • Staff spend less time on coordination and more time on the mission
Possible directions — not requirements
A custom web app
A low-code solution (Airtable, Notion)
Google Workspace automation
An existing volunteer platform, configured well
Something nobody has thought of yet

The goal is not to build software. The goal is to solve the problem.

Making City Budget Data Understandable
Civic org Open for builders
Civic Commons Project
Civic org · San Antonio, TX
Open government advocacy
Serving all city residents
Background

Every year, the city publishes its annual budget — a document that determines how hundreds of millions of dollars get spent on schools, roads, parks, housing, and public safety.

Almost no one reads it. Not because residents don't care. Because it's a 400-page PDF written in bureaucratic language, with no search, no navigation, and no context.

Current workflow
  • City publishes a PDF budget document annually
  • Reporters spend hours manually extracting figures
  • Advocates search the PDF for specific line items
  • Residents who want to engage have no entry point
  • Public comment periods pass with little participation
The problem

The information exists. It's public. It's required by law. But in its current form, it's effectively inaccessible to anyone who isn't a professional researcher or journalist.

Civic participation suffers not because people don't care about how their city spends money — but because the tools to understand it don't exist.

What success looks like
  • A resident can find how much the city spends on parks in under 30 seconds
  • Journalists can quickly compare year-over-year spending changes
  • Community groups can share specific budget breakdowns with their members
  • Public comment participation increases meaningfully
Possible directions — not requirements
An interactive budget explorer website
A visual storytelling page (charts, plain language)
A searchable database with category breakdowns
A plain-language summary generator
A comparison tool across multiple years

The goal is civic understanding. The format is open.

Open challenge · Pilot example
Community Accessibility Mapping
Community group Open for builders
OpenPath Collective
Community group · San Antonio, TX
Disability advocacy & access
Serving residents citywide
Background

For people with mobility challenges, planning a trip across the city requires knowing which entrances are accessible, where ramps exist, which transit stops have working elevators, and which routes are actually navigable.

That information exists — but it's scattered across city government spreadsheets, transit agency databases, and volunteer-compiled notes. There is no single place to find it.

Current workflow
  • Advocates manually compile accessibility data from multiple city sources
  • Data lives in spreadsheets that aren't publicly shared
  • Residents rely on word of mouth or personal experience
  • When infrastructure changes, updates don't happen consistently
  • No centralized reporting mechanism for broken or missing access points
The problem

Residents with disabilities can't reliably plan accessible routes. The data exists — in fragments, across multiple sources — but has never been assembled into something usable.

The result is that people either avoid unfamiliar areas entirely, or discover inaccessible barriers after they've already traveled to get there.

What success looks like
  • A resident can check accessibility before leaving home
  • Community members can report issues and suggest updates
  • Advocates have a shared, maintained data source to reference
  • City officials can see gaps and prioritize improvements
Possible directions — not requirements
A public interactive map (Google Maps, Mapbox, Leaflet)
A mobile-first website for on-the-go lookup
A crowdsourced reporting tool
Data standardization and open dataset publishing
Integration with existing mapping platforms

This might be a map. It might be something else. Start with the need.

Open challenge · Pilot example
Grant Discovery for Small Nonprofits
Nonprofit Open for builders
Meridian Research Group
Nonprofit research · Texas
Community funding advocacy
Supports 40+ local orgs
Background

Small nonprofits — the kind doing direct community service with lean teams — are often the worst positioned to find the funding they're eligible for. They don't have grant writers. They don't have researchers. They have program staff doing three jobs at once.

The grants exist. Community foundations, federal programs, corporate giving initiatives — there is more funding available than most small orgs ever access. The problem is discovery.

Current workflow
  • Staff manually search Google, GrantWatch, Foundation Directory
  • Opportunities are tracked in personal spreadsheets or email folders
  • Deadlines are missed because no alert system exists
  • Organizations don't know what neighboring orgs have already applied for
  • Time spent searching comes directly out of program delivery hours
The problem

Staff at small nonprofits spend significant time searching for grants instead of running programs. Many eligible opportunities are never found. Deadlines are missed. Funding that could reach communities doesn't.

The information exists — but it's fragmented, behind paywalls, or buried in email newsletters that require you to already know where to look.

What success looks like
  • Staff spend less time searching and more time serving
  • Relevant opportunities surface automatically based on org profile
  • Deadline alerts prevent missed applications
  • Small orgs can see what funding peers have accessed
  • More community funding reaches the organizations doing direct work
Possible directions — not requirements
A searchable, filterable grant portal
Email or SMS deadline alerts
AI-based matching by org mission and size
A shared community grant database
A lightweight CRM for tracking applications

A simple alert system might do more good than a complex platform. Start small.

Why we exist

This should be easier than it is.

There are people everywhere who want to use their skills to help — developers, designers, researchers, builders of all kinds. And there are organizations and communities with real problems they can't solve alone.

The gap between those two groups isn't a technical problem. Finding each other just shouldn't be this hard.

Common Ground is open source, nonprofit, and free. No investors, no fees, no algorithms. Think of it as a commons for local problem-solving — open infrastructure for people who want to work on things that matter, close to home.

"A food bank doesn't care if you can reverse a binary tree. They care if you can fix their volunteer signup form." That's the kind of competence Common Ground is built around.

What actually happens

What happens when a challenge is posted.

Common Ground is lightweight by design. No project management software, no formal contracts. Just a clear, human process that keeps things moving.

01

Challenge is posted

An organization describes their problem in plain language. What's broken, what they've tried, what success looks like.

02

Community asks questions

Builders comment, clarify scope, and discuss approaches. The org responds. The problem gets sharper.

03

Builders express interest

One or more builders raise their hand. Solo or as a small team. No formal application — just genuine interest.

04

Work happens in the open

Progress is shared publicly. The org gives feedback. The community can follow along, offer input, or jump in.

05

Challenge is completed

The org confirms the work is useful. The solution — and the story of how it got built — is archived publicly.

06

Story gets shared

What was the problem? Who solved it? What did they build? The community grows every time a challenge closes.
